Exponential Distribution

1. Original Population

The original population follows an Exponential distribution with a rate parameter of λ = 1. It is a continuous distribution that is right-skewed, meaning most values are concentrated near 0, with a long tail extending to the right. Unlike the Standard Normal distribution, the Exponential distribution is not symmetric or bell-shaped.

Theoretical mean: 1

Theoretical standard deviation: 1

2. Monte Carlo Simulation

I used Monte Carlo simulation to examine the sampling distribution of the sample mean for an Exponential population at different sample sizes. For each sample size, I repeatedly generated random samples from an Exponential distribution with a rate of 1 and calculated the mean of each sample.

Sample size tested: 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 100, 110, 120, 130, 140, 150, 200, 210

Number of simulations: 10,000 for each sample size

# sample size to test
sample_sizes <- c(2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 100, 110, 120, 130, 140, 150, 200, 210)

# number of repeated samples
num_simulations <- 10000

# function to simulate sample means
simulate_means <- function(n) {
  replicate(
    num_simulations,
    mean(rexp(n, rate = 1))
  )
}

# run the simulation for every sample size

simulation_results <- lapply(sample_sizes, simulate_means)

# name each result by its sample size
names(simulation_results) <- sample_sizes

3. Visual Evidence

I used histograms and Normal Q-Q plots to examine how the sampling distribution of the sample mean changes as the sample size increases. Since the original Exponential population is strongly right-skewed, I want to see how quickly the sampling distributions become more symmetric and bell-shaped.

The histograms show the distribution of 10,000 sample means for each sample size. The red curves represent the theoretical Normal approximations based on the population mean and standard error. I use these graphs to examine whether the sampling distributions become less right-skewed and follow the Normal curve more closely as n increases.

4. Numerical Evidence

In addition to the histograms and Q-Q plots, I calculated numerical values to check how close the sampling distributions are to normal. I compared the simulated means and standard errors with their theoretical values. I also calculated skewness and excess kurtosis to see how the shape of the sampling distributions changes as the sample size increases.

# function to calculate skewness
skew <- function(x) {
  m3 <- mean((x - mean(x))^3)
  m2 <- mean((x - mean(x))^2)
  m3 / m2^(3/2)
}

# function to calculate excess kurtosis
kurt <- function(x) {
  m4 <- mean((x - mean(x))^4)
  m2 <- mean((x - mean(x))^2)
  m4 / m2^2 - 3
}

5. Approximately Normal?

Selected sample size (n): 30

I consider the sampling distribution approximately normal at n = 30. From the histograms, I notice that the distribution becomes more symmetric and bell-shaped, and the bars follow the red Normal curve fairly closely. The Q-Q plot also shows that most of the points follow the reference line, although there are still some deviations at the tails.

I considered n = 20 because its histogram already looks fairly bell-shaped. The numerical results at n = 20 are also promising, with skewness of 0.400 and excess kurtosis of 0.123. However, the Q-Q plot still shows noticeable curvature, especially at the tails. At n = 30, the points follow the reference line more closely, which makes me more comfortable calling the distribution approximately normal.

The numerical evidence also supports this. At n = 30, the simulated mean is 1.001, which is very close to the theoretical mean of 1. The simulated and theoretical standard errors are both 0.183, showing that the simulation agrees with the expected variability of the sample means. The skewness is 0.354 and excess kurtosis is 0.194. Although these values are not exactly 0 and some right skewness remains, they are much closer to 0 compared to smaller sample sizes such as n = 2.

I also noticed that n = 40 and n = 50 follow the Normal curve and Q-Q reference line more closely. However, the goal is to find the smallest sample size that I can call approximately normal, rather than finding the closest or most perfect fit. Based on both the visual and numerical evidence, I’d say the sampling distribution is approximately normal at n = 30 for the Exponential population.

6. Limitations and Surprises

One limitation of this experiment is that deciding when the sampling distribution becomes approximately normal is partly based on visual judgment. Although I selected n = 30, there is no exact cutoff for when a distribution is normal enough. The results are also based on 10,000 simulations, so the graphs and numerical values may change slightly with a different random seed.

I found it interesting that the sampling distribution became more symmetric as the sample size increased, even though the original Exponential population was strongly right-skewed. I also noticed that the histograms started looking approximately normal before the Q-Q plots followed the reference line closely. This shows that different ways of checking normality may lead to slightly different judgement about the smallest acceptable sample size.

7. Conclusion

The Exponential experiment showed that the sampling distribution of the sample mean can become approximately normal even when the original population is strongly right-skewed. Based on the visual and numerical evidence, I selected n = 30 as the smallest sample size where I felt comfortable calling the sampling distribution approximately normal.

As the sample size increased, the histograms became more symmetric and bell-shaped, and the Q-Q plots followed the reference line more closely. The standard error also decreased, meaning the sample means became more concentrated around the theoretical mean of 1. This supports the Central Limit Theorem, which explains that as the sample size increases, the distribution of sample means becomes closer to a Normal distribution.

Compared to the Standard Normal and Six-Sided Die experiments, the Exponential population needed a larger sample size before I considered its sampling distribution approximately normal. This shows that the original population’s shape matters when deciding how large n should be, and that n = 30 is not a universal requirement for every distribution.
